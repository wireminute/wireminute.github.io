Starship Reaches Orbit as Booster Catch Is Called Off

The world's biggest rocket flew to orbit on Friday, but its booster never came home to the launch tower, after an unspecified problem forced controllers to call off the catch and send it into the sea.

The 124-metre rocket lifted off from the company's Starbase site in southern Texas on Friday afternoon, its 15th test flight, powered by 33 engines on the Super Heavy booster. About two and a half minutes after launch, the upper stage fired its own engines and separated, heading for orbit while the booster turned back toward the launch site.

The plan was to catch both parts of the rocket. The booster was meant to be plucked from the air by mechanical arms on the launch tower, a first for this upgraded version of the vehicle. Instead, an unspecified problem forced controllers to call off the catch and send the booster to a splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico, where it erupted in a fireball as it hit the water about six and a half minutes after liftoff. A section of the booster was left sticking out of the sea.

The company did not immediately say what went wrong. A company spokesman said it was not yet clear why the booster could not be brought back, and officials stressed that protecting the launch site and the surrounding area takes priority over any recovery attempt. The booster had built-in safety checks that divert it away from the tower if the conditions for a catch are not met.

The upper stage, the part that gives the rocket its name, completed its climb without trouble. Its engines fired a second time to place it in orbit about 275 kilometres above Earth, and it deployed 26 of the company's newest satellites over about 42 minutes. It was expected to circle the planet about 14 times over more than 22 hours.

Attention now turns to Saturday, when the company hopes to bring the spacecraft itself back to the launch site and catch it in mid-air, something never done before with a vehicle returning from orbit. The attempt is planned for about 3:20 p.m. local time. If the catch looks difficult on final approach, the spacecraft will instead splash down in the Gulf.

The flight was the boldest test yet of a rocket designed to be fully reusable. The company has caught the booster three times before, but never this upgraded version, built as the production model for future Moon and Mars missions. A double catch of both stages in a single flight remains unachieved.

Key points

  • Starship's 15th test flight reached orbit on Friday after lifting off from Texas, but the Super Heavy booster catch was aborted.
  • The booster was diverted to the Gulf of Mexico, where it erupted in flames on impact; the cause of the abort is not yet known.
  • The upper stage deployed 26 satellites and is due to circle Earth about 14 times before a return attempt on Saturday.
  • The company hopes to catch the returning spacecraft in mid-air at the launch site, a first for a vehicle coming back from orbit.
