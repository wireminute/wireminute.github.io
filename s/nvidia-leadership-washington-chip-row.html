Nvidia Hunts for a New Leader as Washington Fights Over Chip Sales to China

The world's most valuable chipmaker is searching for a new chief executive while a public row inside the United States government — over how its processors reached a Singapore firm at the centre of a spying scandal — spills into the open.

Jensen Huang, the man who built Nvidia into the defining company of the artificial intelligence age, stepped down as chief executive on Thursday. His exit followed revelations about extramarital affairs, including one involving a businesswoman suspected of spying for China, and it has thrown the chipmaker into the biggest crisis of its history.

The company's finance chief, Colette Kress, has taken over as interim chief executive, and Huang is expected to testify before Congress. The board, meanwhile, is understood to have approached Rene Haas, the chief executive of British chip designer Arm, about succeeding him. Haas spent seven years at Nvidia early in his career, rising to run its computing products business, before leaving for Arm in 2013 and taking its top job in 2022. Whether Haas is formally offered the job — and whether he would take it — remains an open question, and neither company has commented publicly on the approach.

On Friday the fallout reached the cabinet table. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly accused the Commerce Department of sitting on a decision to blacklist Megaspeed, the Singapore firm whose name runs through the whole affair, saying his own department and the defence, state and trade offices had wanted the company blacklisted for months, while the authority to act rested with Commerce. He said he was frustrated, noting the president had made it very clear China should not receive Nvidia chips above the H100 level.

Megaspeed, where the woman at the centre of the scandal held an executive role, has been under investigation in Singapore since February last year over possible false declarations connected to purchases of servers. Washington already restricts sales of the company's most powerful AI processors to China, and lawmakers from both parties are now pressing to tighten the rules further, including a gap that lets Chinese companies hire computing capacity from data centres abroad and run the restricted chips from afar. New licensing rules for countries such as Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Singapore are among the options being discussed.

Investors are voting with their feet. Nvidia shares fell more than 3 percent on Friday, while Arm's American-listed shares were down more than 5 percent. What began as a personal scandal now sits at the crossroads of corporate governance, trade policy and the global race for AI supremacy, and its outcome will be felt far beyond one company.

Key points

  • Jensen Huang stepped down on Thursday; finance chief Colette Kress is interim chief executive.
  • The board has approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas about the top job, with no deal guaranteed.
  • The treasury secretary publicly blamed the commerce department for failing to blacklist Singapore firm Megaspeed.
  • Lawmakers want tougher chip export controls, including closing the overseas cloud-rental loophole.
