US Loses Measles Elimination Status After 26 Years as Cases Hit Three-Decade High

The United States has lost its measles elimination status after 26 years, after health officials found the virus had spread continuously in the country for more than a year, with cases this year at their highest in more than three decades.

The Pan American Health Organization, the Americas branch of the World Health Organization, declared measles endemic in the United States on Tuesday. An expert panel advising the organisation recommended that the elimination status be withdrawn, and its director, Jarbas Barbosa, said he had accepted the recommendation. The status, held since 2000, is lost when the same strain of the virus spreads from person to person for 12 months or longer. Bolivia and Mexico lost their elimination status in the same review.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has confirmed 5,412 measles cases in the United States this year, as of November 12, across 45 states and the capital, in 43 outbreaks. It is the highest annual total since 1991, and six deaths have been reported. Around 7,700 infections have been recorded since the start of 2025. Most cases have been among people who were not vaccinated, and health officials say the true number of infections is likely higher than the confirmed count.

Vaccination coverage has slipped. Kindergarten coverage has fallen to 92.4 percent, and two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine are 97 percent effective at preventing the disease. To regain its elimination status, the United States must stop the continuous spread of the virus for at least 12 months, a test that starts from the last chain of transmission.

The director of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Erica Schwartz, said last week that the agency would not change its measles response if the status was lost, and officials said on Tuesday that 100 million dollars had been invested in state and local health departments to reach the communities where transmission is occurring. A former senior immunisation official at the agency called elimination a vital sign for the public health system, warning that losing it reflects wider gaps in protection.
