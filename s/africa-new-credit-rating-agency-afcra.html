Africa's New Credit Rating Agency Aims to Rewrite How the Continent Is Judged

Africa now has its own credit rating agency, launched to challenge the three Western firms that decide what the continent's governments pay to borrow, after years of complaints that their ratings overstate African risk.

The Africa Credit Rating Agency, known as AfCRA, was officially launched on October 7 in Port Louis, Mauritius, where it is headquartered, bringing to fruition an initiative African leaders first endorsed in 2017 and the African Union approved in 2018. The agency is independent, privately owned and self-funded, with governments barred from holding shares, and it aims to complement rather than replace Moody's, S&P Global and Fitch, the three Western firms that dominate global ratings.

Its interim chief executive, Sifiso Falala, says the agency is already in discussions with 15 African governments, with research planned in Mauritius, Kenya, Togo, the Seychelles and Burundi, and its first sovereign ratings are expected by the end of March. Beyond governments, it plans to assess companies, municipalities, banks and local-currency debt, as well as multilateral lenders including Afreximbank and private financial institutions.

The case for it is the cost of borrowing. Only 32 of Africa's 55 countries hold ratings from the big three agencies, leaving 23 with no rating at all, and only three African sovereigns hold investment-grade ratings. The average African sovereign rating is B or B-, against BB for other emerging regions, and persistent risk premiums are estimated to cost Africa about 75 billion dollars a year in excess interest payments. In 2024, Africa paid about nine dollars for every 100 borrowed on international markets, against about 4.70 dollars in Asia and 6.50 dollars in Latin America.

Supporters say local knowledge will produce fairer assessments. "A significant number of African countries carry speculative-grade ratings despite having no history of sovereign default," Falala said. Mauritius minister Jyoti Jeetun said: "AfCRA is not about asking the world to rate Africa more favourably. It is about helping the world understand Africa more accurately." African Union Commission chair Mahmoud Ali Youssouf called the agency an "important pillar" of the continent's financial architecture.

The test will be credibility. The ratings are solicited, meaning governments pay for assessments and give access to officials and financial information, and the agency is owned by Plus 94 Research, a South African research company co-founded by Falala, which plans to raise 15 million dollars through a share sale that would give African investors a two-thirds stake. Analysts say the first proof will be whether the agency is willing to downgrade an African government, and whether investors outside Africa listen when its first ratings land in March.

Key points

  • AfCRA launched on October 7 in Port Louis, Mauritius, as an independent, privately owned agency backed by the African Union.
  • It is in talks with 15 African governments and aims to publish its first sovereign ratings by the end of March.
  • Only 32 of 55 African countries are rated by the big three agencies, and risk premiums are estimated to cost Africa about 75 billion dollars a year in extra interest.
  • The agency will rate governments, companies, municipalities and banks on a solicited basis; its credibility will rest on whether investors trust its first ratings.
