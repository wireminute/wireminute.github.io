Colombia's President Blames Ex-FARC Rebels for Villavicencio Car Bomb

Colombia's president has blamed former FARC rebels for the car bomb that wrecked the Meta governor's office in Villavicencio, naming a rebel commander he said plotted the attack in revenge for government offensives. At least 23 people were hurt.

The blast went off late on Monday near Plaza Los Libertadores, wrecking the front of the governor's office and damaging shops and buildings nearby. Health authorities in Meta said on Tuesday that 23 people had been hurt, including a child, and that some of the injuries were serious. Two members of the governor's office staff were among the hurt, along with five security guards working at the headquarters. No deaths had been reported by Tuesday morning. Police figures cited in some reports put the number of injured at 25. The head of national police operations said initial tests showed the car had carried between 80 and 100 kilograms of pentolite, a high explosive used in military warheads. The explosion damaged more than 80 buildings within about 500 metres, destroyed two shops including a two-storey cafe that collapsed, wrecked at least three vehicles and cut power in the area.

President Abelardo De La Espriella, visiting Villavicencio on Tuesday, blamed Antonio Medina, a commander of the Central General Staff, a dissident group of former FARC fighters, saying Medina plotted the bombing in retaliation for government offensives in the province. "The bandit Antonio Medina from the [FARC] dissidents is to blame, and we're going to go after him and make him pay with blood," he told reporters. He said drug-trafficking groups were lashing out because the state was defeating them. No group has said it carried out the attack, so the blame rests on the president's statement and on investigators' initial theories. Villavicencio's mayor, Alexander Baquero, had told local radio he believed a FARC holdout group operating in Meta province was behind the attack, seeking revenge for military strikes against it. Meta Governor Rafaela Cortes said she was coordinating emergency and security measures with the army and police.

The attack came on the eve of the tenth anniversary of the 2016 peace deal between the Colombian state and the FARC, which laid down its weapons the following year. Shortly before the blast, the president had denounced the accord as a false peace and rejected the previous government's policy of negotiated settlements with armed groups. It follows a car bomb at the Pipiral toll on the Bogota-Villavicencio road on November 15, which damaged buildings there but left no one dead or hurt. The story is still developing.

Key points

  • President De La Espriella blamed ex-FARC rebels for the Villavicencio car bomb, naming dissident commander Antonio Medina as the man who plotted it.
  • At least 23 people were hurt, health authorities said, including a child; police figures cited in some reports put the number at 25. No deaths have been reported.
  • Police said the car carried 80 to 100 kilograms of pentolite; the blast damaged more than 80 buildings and destroyed two shops, including a cafe that collapsed.
  • No group has claimed the attack; it came on the eve of the tenth anniversary of Colombia's 2016 peace deal with the FARC.
