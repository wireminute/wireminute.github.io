Houthi Strike Hits Riyadh's Tallest Tower, Home of Saudi Wealth Fund

A Houthi missile strike hit Riyadh's tallest skyscraper on Thursday, blowing out windows across seven to eight floors of the tower that houses Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund, in the sharpest blow yet to the capital's financial district.

Saudi civil defence said the attack caused material damage to the 80-storey Public Investment Fund Tower and nearby buildings, but no injuries were reported. The tower, 385 metres tall, dominates the King Abdullah Financial District, the showcase for the kingdom's economic ambitions.

Photographs released by state media showed the tower's bent metal facade and shattered glass. Entrances to the financial district were closed and people were evacuated as ambulances and fire trucks reached the scene. The Houthis claimed responsibility hours later, saying they had targeted the financial district twice during the day, first before dawn and again in the evening, and also claimed attacks on the capital's airport, a petrochemical facility in Jubail and an air base in Khamis Mushait.

It was the second time in less than two weeks that a Houthi attack damaged a landmark in the financial district, after the state oil company's building was hit on November 8. The Saudi-led coalition said it had dealt with a missile threat aimed at Riyadh and intercepted several ballistic missiles fired towards the southern region, and said it destroyed a launchpad used in the attacks. Saudi aircraft also struck the Houthi-held Yemeni capital, Sanaa, in the early hours of Thursday, according to the Houthi-run news agency.

Saudi Arabia's foreign ministry condemned the attacks on civilian infrastructure, and Pakistan also condemned the strike on the tower. The Houthis, aligned with Iran, have stepped up attacks in recent weeks as the wider regional war has spread. The World Health Organization puts the toll from the latest escalation at 2,260 killed and 10,519 wounded. Damage teams are still assessing the scene in Riyadh. The story is still developing.
