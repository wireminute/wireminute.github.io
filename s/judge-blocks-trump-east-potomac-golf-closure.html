Judge Halts Closure of Historic Washington Golf Course in Blow to Trump Redevelopment Plan

A federal judge has temporarily blocked the Trump administration from closing a historic public golf course in Washington on Sunday, ruling that the government's stated reasons for shutting it down did not hold up.

US District Judge Ana Reyes issued the order late on Saturday, hours before East Potomac Park and its golf course were due to close for about 15 months. The administration had planned to rebuild the site, which sits near the Jefferson Memorial about two miles south of the White House, into a single championship-level 18-hole course. In a 30-page decision, the judge found the administration had likely broken environmental and administrative law in its rush to meet what she called an arbitrary deadline, and said the safety reasons given for the closure were a pretext.

The ruling came in a case brought by the DC Preservation League and two local golfers, who have challenged the project since February. They argue the overhaul would change the character of a public course that has served ordinary Washington residents for more than a century and put it out of reach for many of them. The course opened in 1920 and is among the city's earliest public golf courses. The park today has three courses totalling 36 holes, a miniature golf course and a paved loop used by cyclists and runners.

President Donald Trump announced last month that the site would close on November 15 so work could begin, with the Interior Department leading the project and architect Tom Fazio designing the new course. He has said the rebuilt course could host major events such as the US Open and the Ryder Cup while remaining open to the public. The judge's order keeps the park open during its normal hours through November 28, but does not require the government to keep running golf there, and repairs and planning work can continue.

The Justice Department said after the ruling that it would keep defending the administration's agenda in court, and noted that nothing in the order stops planning for a world-class course from going ahead. The decision is the latest court setback for the president's efforts to reshape the capital's historic public spaces, and the case now returns to court with the closure on hold.
