AI Boom Breathes New Life Into San Francisco, but Fears of a Crash Are Growing

San Francisco is enjoying an artificial intelligence boom that is breathing new life into a city hollowed out by the pandemic, but some of the people building the technology now fear the sector is heading for a fall.

The city saw residents leave during the pandemic, and remote working left its downtown weakened. AI has since become the main engine of its recovery. OpenAI and Anthropic, the company behind the Claude chatbot, are based in the city, and Anthropic listed on Nasdaq last week. Rents and house prices are rising, office vacancies are falling, and buses and billboards carry AI advertisements.

But what if AI share prices fall sharply and investment drops with them? The International Monetary Fund warned last month that such a correction was a possibility. Concerns have persisted since the scandal around Jensen Huang, the former boss of chipmaker Nvidia, pushed AI stocks lower, even though Nvidia remains highly profitable, with quarterly profits of $65bn, double a year earlier.

One AI worker at one of the city's largest companies, who asked not to be named, said he was certain a crash was coming. He doubted leading US AI firms could earn enough to justify their huge spending as cheaper, more capable models emerge, especially from China. A second worker said a significant correction was entirely possible, but that many colleagues were more worried about the existential risk AI could pose to humanity.

A crash would hit San Francisco harder than Silicon Valley, because the boom is concentrated in the city itself. One study found 60% of the venture capital invested in American AI firms between 2023 and 2025 went to companies based in the city. Yet hiring has not kept pace: Jeff Bellisario of the Bay Area Council Economic Institute said hiring had not matched the investment, and Ted Egan, the city's chief economist, said the city was not seeing large overall tech job growth.

Enrico Moretti, an economics professor at the University of California, Berkeley, puts the city about 35,000 jobs below its pre-pandemic peak, with much AI spending going to data centres outside the Bay Area. He warned a significant correction could be very painful, with the main effect felt in service jobs, from restaurants and shops to dog walking, while rising vacancies could weigh on rents and house prices. Egan does not expect a slowdown to change the city's direction, and healthcare and tourism are growing too. Lauren Goldboss, who runs a small dog-walking firm, has been hiring staff and buying vans as her office service for AI start-ups grows.

Key points

  • San Francisco is enjoying an AI-driven revival, with rising rents and house prices, falling office vacancies and thousands of new start-ups
  • The IMF warned last month of a possible sharp fall in AI share prices and investment, and workers inside the industry fear a correction is coming
  • 60% of US AI venture capital from 2023 to 2025 went to San Francisco firms, yet the city is still about 35,000 jobs below its pre-pandemic peak
  • Economists warn a correction would hit service jobs hardest, but say healthcare and tourism growth may cushion the blow
