US Flights Unlikely to Run Normally Until Late Sunday, Aviation Chief Says

American air travel is unlikely to run to its normal timetable until late on Sunday, the head of the country's aviation regulator said, as airlines worked through a second day of cancellations after a nationwide shutdown set off by a faulty router installation.

Federal Aviation Administration chief Bryan Bedford said on Saturday that he expected timetables to be largely back to normal by late Sunday, and apologised for the disruption, adding that the system remains stable. By Saturday night, airlines had cancelled more than 6,400 flights and delayed more than 8,000, according to tracking site FlightAware, and 875 flights scheduled for Sunday were already cancelled. That followed about 9,200 cancellations and 7,500 delays on Friday, when the regulator halted nearly all departing flights for almost five hours, from 3:54 p.m. to 8:15 p.m. Eastern — the longest countrywide halt since the 2001 attacks.

Transport Secretary Sean Duffy said the failure began during work to swap ageing copper lines for fibre at a remote air traffic site, where contractor L3Harris fitted a new router. The router began flooding other routers with data — firing off more than a thousand connection attempts every few seconds — and knocked out communications and radar feeds at 17 of the regulator's 22 en route centres, which guide aircraft during the cruise phase of flight. A union representing aviation safety staff said the trouble was isolated to two routers updated on Friday, believed to be in Arkansas and Texas.

Duffy said investigators were still working out whether the fault lay in installer error or in a faulty piece of equipment, and that no conclusion had been reached. L3Harris said it was taking the matter seriously and supporting the investigation, and the regulator said it was carrying out extra testing with the company to check for further risks.

The shutdown prompted an emergency meeting at the White House on Friday as federal agencies scrambled to establish whether the failure was a cyberattack. Officials said there was no evidence of anything nefarious. The regulator has already replaced 68 percent of its legacy copper telecom network with high-speed fibre, and Duffy has called for a 50-billion-dollar overhaul, saying the old equipment cannot cope with modern demands. Because the regulator caused the delays, airlines are not required to pay compensation to stranded passengers, and travellers have been urged to check their flight status before heading to the airport.

Key points

  • The aviation regulator's chief says normal flight schedules are unlikely to return until late on Sunday; he apologised and said the system remains stable.
  • More than 6,400 flights were cancelled on Saturday and over 8,000 delayed, after about 9,200 cancellations on Friday; 875 Sunday flights were already cancelled.
  • The failure was traced to a contractor-installed router flooding the network during copper-to-fibre work, cutting communications at 17 of 22 en route centres.
  • Investigators have not determined whether the cause was installer error or faulty equipment; officials say there is no evidence of a cyberattack.
