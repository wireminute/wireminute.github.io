US Flights Resume After Longest Nationwide Ground Stop in 25 Years

Flights resumed across the United States late on Friday after the aviation regulator lifted a nationwide ground stop that had held every departing plane on the ground for more than four hours, leaving about 1.5 million passengers facing delays and cancellations.

The regulator lifted the stop at 8:15 p.m. Eastern on Friday, about four hours after ordering every departing flight in the country to stay on the ground. Departures began moving again, but officials warned the backlog would take days to clear, with aircraft and crews left out of position across the country.

The trouble began in the afternoon, when air traffic control centres saw instability in their radar and communications systems. Engineers had pushed a routine software update shortly before, and the ageing switches and routers — copper lines and analogue equipment dating to the 1980s — choked on the sudden surge of data and started returning inconsistent readings. Officials shut the system down rather than risk flying on unreliable information. The transport secretary blamed the ageing equipment and called on Congress to fund a $50 billion overhaul of the air traffic control system, while the homeland security secretary said new technology introduced into the old system had overwhelmed it.

The order, issued at 3:54 p.m. Eastern, first halted traffic in Chicago, a busy connecting hub, before widening nationwide. More than 4,000 flights were already in the air, heading for American airports, when it landed; they were allowed to continue to their destinations, and officials said every flight had been accounted for. Flights arriving from overseas were handled one by one, and Alaska and Hawaii were not affected.

Officials were quick to rule out foul play. The homeland security secretary said there was no evidence of a cyberattack or a rogue artificial intelligence system behind the failure, and investigators said they did not believe criminal activity was involved. The software company whose own widespread outage was under way the same day said its services were unrelated to the ground stop.

The scale of the disruption was vast. Tracking data counted more than 6,500 flights cancelled and about 8,800 delayed, with about 1.5 million passengers affected and more than 800 flights diverted. About 700 flights had already been cancelled for Saturday. It was the first such order since January 2023, when a computer failure halted flights, and the longest such shutdown in 25 years.

Airlines warned the disruption would linger. One carrier cancelled its remaining Friday evening flights and warned of more on Saturday, while another said cancellations would continue as schedules were rebuilt. The transport secretary said he expected normal operations to return before the busy Thanksgiving travel period.

Key points

  • The nationwide ground stop was lifted at 8:15 p.m. Eastern on Friday, about four hours after every departing flight in the country was ordered to stay on the ground.
  • Officials blame overloaded switches and routers in an ageing air traffic control system; a routine software update pushed in the afternoon overwhelmed the old equipment.
  • There is no evidence of a cyberattack, officials said, and the failure was unrelated to the software company's own outage the same day.
  • More than 6,500 flights were cancelled and about 8,800 delayed, affecting about 1.5 million passengers; airlines warn disruption will continue into the weekend.
