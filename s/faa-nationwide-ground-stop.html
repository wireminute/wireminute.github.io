Overloaded Routers and Ageing Kit Behind US Flight Ground Stop, Officials Say

The hours-long nationwide grounding of American flights was caused by overloaded switches and routers in an ageing air traffic control system, officials said on Friday night, as the stop was lifted and departures began moving again.

The regulator lifted the stop at 8:15 p.m. Eastern on Friday, about four hours after ordering every departing flight in the country to stay on the ground. The transport secretary said American airspace was open again, but warned that schedules would take time to recover, with aircraft and crews left out of position across the country.

The trouble began at 3:36 p.m., when air traffic control centres saw instability in their radar and communications systems. Switches and routers became overloaded with data and started returning inconsistent readings, and officials shut the system down rather than risk flying on unreliable information. The transport secretary called it a failure of a legacy system — old switches, old routers and copper lines from the 1980s — while the homeland security secretary said new technology introduced into the old system had overwhelmed it. One report pointed to a telecommunications network overseen by a defence contractor, though the regulator has not confirmed that.

The order had landed just before 4 p.m. Eastern, first halting traffic in Chicago, a busy connecting hub, before widening nationwide. Aircraft already in the air were allowed to continue to their destinations, flights arriving from overseas were handled one by one, and officials said every flight had been accounted for. The regulator described the problem as a temporary telecommunications outage and said its technicians were investigating.

Officials were quick to rule out foul play. The homeland security secretary said there was no evidence so far of a cyberattack or a rogue AI system behind the failure, and the software company whose own widespread outage was under way the same day said its services were unrelated to the ground stop.

The scale of the disruption was vast. Flight-tracking data counted more than 6,600 flights cancelled and 8,600 delayed, with another 1,500 already cancelled for Sunday. It was the first nationwide stop since a computer-system failure in January 2023, and only the second since the September 11 attacks — the longest such shutdown since then. The transport secretary said he expected the system to be back up and running in time for the busy Thanksgiving travel period.
