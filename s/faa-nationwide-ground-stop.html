Trump Says He Bears No Blame for US Flight Chaos as Airlines Recover

President Donald Trump said on Monday he took no responsibility for the nationwide flight ground stop that stranded thousands of travellers, blaming decades-old equipment and his predecessor as airlines worked to restore normal schedules.

President Donald Trump said on Monday he took no responsibility for the nationwide ground stop that stranded thousands of travellers, blaming ageing equipment and his predecessor. Speaking as he left the White House for a trip to Asia, he said he had spent the crisis in the Oval Office. "I don't take any responsibility," he said. "They did a great job."

Trump described the failure as very old equipment suffering what he called a heart attack, blaming four decades of failure to modernise the air traffic system. He said his predecessor's administration had made the system worse and singled out its transport secretary, adding that everyone had been kept safe and no aircraft had crashed. It was his first public comment on the disruption since Friday. He had stayed silent through the weekend except for a Sunday online post blaming the previous administration and urging Congress to fund repairs.

His transport secretary, Sean Duffy, is seeking about 50 billion dollars from Congress to overhaul a system he says has been neglected for decades. Duffy made the decision on Friday to halt departures and has led the public response alongside the homeland security secretary. Trump said he had been briefed within half an hour of the stop taking effect.

By Sunday afternoon, airlines had cancelled more than 1,600 flights and delayed almost 3,600, according to tracking site FlightAware, taking the total number scrapped since Friday past 17,000. JetBlue cancelled more than a quarter of its Sunday flights and American Airlines about 13 percent. Duffy said on Sunday that operations should be back to normal on Monday, a day later than the aviation regulator's chief had first predicted.

Investigators have traced the failure to two routers installed during work to replace ageing copper lines with fibre at remote air traffic sites, one in El Dorado, Arkansas, and a second in Amarillo, Texas. The new equipment flooded the network with data, knocking out communications and radar feeds at 17 of the regulator's 22 en route centres. The regulator has switched off the routers involved and about 60 similar devices while the investigation continues. Officials say there is no evidence of a cyberattack, and it is still not known whether the fault lay in installer error or in a faulty piece of equipment. Because the regulator caused the delays, airlines are not required to pay compensation to stranded passengers.

Key points

  • Trump said on Monday he took no responsibility for the ground stop, blaming decades-old equipment and the previous administration.
  • It was his first public comment since Friday; he had stayed silent through the weekend apart from a Sunday online post urging Congress to fund repairs.
  • Transport Secretary Sean Duffy is seeking about 50 billion dollars from Congress for a full overhaul of the air traffic system.
  • More than 17,000 flights have been cancelled since Friday; the failure is traced to routers in Arkansas and Texas, with no evidence of a cyberattack.
