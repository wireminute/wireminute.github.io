Somalia Says International Support Saved It as UN Mission Closes and Peacekeeper Funding Falters

Somalia's foreign affairs minister says his country would not have recovered from three decades of conflict without the international community, as the United Nations closes its political mission and funding for African peacekeepers runs into trouble.

Ali Mohamed Omar, Somalia's State Minister for Foreign Affairs, said in an interview published on Sunday that Somalia has come a long way since its government collapsed more than 30 years ago and plunged the country into civil war. He said the country would not have recovered without help from the international community, and he hopes that support, including financial backing, will continue as Somalia tries to become a stronger state.

The United Nations political mission in Somalia closed on 31 October, ending roughly two decades of continuous UN political engagement in the country. The closure completed a two-year transition requested by the Somali government, which says it wants to take responsibility for its own affairs. The mission's work, including support for constitutional reform, elections, human rights and the rule of law, has passed to Somali institutions, the UN country team and other partners. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres marked the closure by calling for sustained international assistance.

African Union peacekeepers remain on the ground, supporting Mogadishu in its fight against al-Shabaab, an al-Qaeda-linked armed group that controls large parts of central and southern Somalia. About 12,000 troops serve under the African Union Support and Stabilisation Mission, known as AUSSOM. But the mission faces a funding cliff: the United States told the African Union in July that it would stop financing the UN logistics office sustaining the mission after 31 December, and it has blocked a UN funding framework that would have covered up to 75 percent of the mission's costs. Uganda, the first country to send troops in 2007, began withdrawing its forces on 2 November and plans to complete its exit by the end of 2027.

Somalia also faces political friction at home. The opposition says the president is not legitimate, while the government says the new constitution allows him to stay in office until next year. Omar said that despite these issues, Somalia is more stable than it was a few years ago.

Key points

  • Somalia's foreign minister says the country would not have recovered from 30 years of conflict without international help.
  • The UN political mission closed on 31 October after a two-year transition requested by Mogadishu.
  • About 12,000 African Union troops still back Mogadishu against al-Shabaab, but US funding for their logistics ends on 31 December.
  • Uganda began pulling its troops out on 2 November, and the opposition disputes the president's legitimacy at home.
