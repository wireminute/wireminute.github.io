Danish Supreme Court Rules 'Ghetto Law' Evictions Were Not Discrimination

Denmark's Supreme Court has ruled that evicting tenants under the country's so-called 'ghetto law' was not discrimination, backing a housing policy at the heart of the country's tough approach to immigration.

The court on Monday rejected an appeal by seven evicted tenants and upheld a 2022 decision by a lower court. The tenants had challenged the termination of their leases by the housing association Boliggaarden, which ended 91 tenancies in the Nøjsomhed district of Helsingor, north of Copenhagen, to keep the area from being classed as a 'hard ghetto' under the law.

The judges found that the law's dividing line, between 'immigrants and descendants from non-Western countries' and everyone else, is a neutral criterion rather than a measure of ethnic origin. They noted that both groups contain people of a very large majority of different ethnic origins, and that no information had been presented about the ethnic background of the residents who were evicted.

The court also said that even if the housing association had aimed to bring the share of non-Western residents in Nøjsomhed to 50 percent or below, that would not amount to direct discrimination, because the criterion itself is lawful. It found no evidence that particular ethnic groups had been put at a disadvantage.

The 2018 law, known in Denmark as the 'ghetto law', allows the relocation of residents from areas where more than half of residents are 'non-Western', a policy the government says combats segregation and so-called parallel societies. The European Union's top court said last year that the law could amount to discrimination if ethnic origin was a decisive consideration when it was adopted, but left the final decision to the national courts. Housing Minister Jacob Mark said he was satisfied with the ruling, while Eddie Khawaja, the lawyer for the evicted residents, said the court had looked more at the wording of the law than at how it was implemented on the ground.

Key points

  • Denmark's Supreme Court ruled that ending 91 leases under the 2018 'ghetto law' was not discrimination on ethnic grounds.
  • The court found the law's 'non-Western' criterion neutral rather than a measure of ethnic origin, and said aiming to cut the share to 50 percent or below was not direct discrimination.
  • The ruling upheld a 2022 lower-court decision in seven cases brought by tenants of the Nøjsomhed area in Helsingor.
  • The EU's top court said last year the law could amount to discrimination if ethnic origin was decisive when it was adopted; Denmark's government welcomed the ruling, while the tenants' lawyer criticised its focus on the law's wording.
