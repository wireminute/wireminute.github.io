Hera Images Reveal Huge Crater on Asteroid Struck in Planetary Defence Test

The first close-up images of the asteroid deliberately struck by a spacecraft in a planetary defence test show a crater almost as large as the asteroid itself, four years after the impact.

The pictures come from the European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft, which has arrived at the Didymos system to examine the aftermath of NASA's DART mission. In September 2022, DART was driven into Dimorphos, a moonlet about 150 metres wide, to test whether a spacecraft could knock a space rock off course. The new images show the blow left a vast hollow spanning nearly the whole width of the small body, settling a question scientists had argued over since the crash: whether the impact would carve out a clear crater or reshape the rubble-pile asteroid altogether.

DART struck Dimorphos at about 22,000 kilometres per hour, throwing a trail of debris some 10,000 kilometres long into space. The collision shortened the moonlet's orbit around the larger asteroid Didymos, about 730 metres wide, by around 33 minutes, from 11 hours 55 minutes. It also changed the way Dimorphos moves: once locked so it always showed the same face to Didymos, it now tumbles chaotically as it circles its companion.

Hera's images also show Didymos itself in new detail, with boulder fields where rocks range from about three metres to ten metres across, the size of cars and buses, on a world whose surface gravity is about 40,000 times weaker than Earth's. The spacecraft will spend six months studying the pair, and two shoebox-sized companion satellites will fly closer to the impact site and eventually land on the asteroid. One of its main jobs is to measure Dimorphos's mass, the figure that determines how much push a future deflection would need.

The test matters because a rock the size of Dimorphos could destroy a city if it hit Earth, while larger ones could cause devastation on a global scale. DART proved a spacecraft can shift an asteroid's path; Hera's measurements will show how efficiently that push was delivered. A new telescope launching in 2027 will hunt for hazardous objects, and missions are already planned to the asteroid Apophis, which will pass about 32,000 kilometres from Earth in 2029.

Key points

  • Hera's first close-ups of Dimorphos show a crater spanning almost the whole width of the 150-metre asteroid.
  • NASA's DART hit the moonlet in 2022 at about 22,000 km/h, shortening its orbit around Didymos by around 33 minutes.
  • The impact left Dimorphos tumbling chaotically and threw a debris trail about 10,000 km long into space.
  • Hera will spend six months measuring the asteroid's mass to calibrate future planetary defence.
