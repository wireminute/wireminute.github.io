Hoax Bomb Threats Spread to Broadcasters and Flights as Europe's Wave Widens

The month-long wave of hoax bomb threats across Europe has spread from schools to newsrooms and air traffic, with broadcasters evacuated, passenger jets diverted and fighter jets scrambled to escort planes threatened in mid-air.

The threats reached newsrooms on 6 November, when broadcasters received bomb warnings. Ireland's RTE evacuated its Dublin headquarters, replacing live programmes with pre-recorded output, while France's TF1 and Bulgaria's Nova were also targeted. At least 12 media organisations were hit around the same time, and Britain's Sky said it had received a security threat but stayed on air. An email to European broadcasters that day claimed to come from the leader of the group behind the plane hoaxes, saying bombs had been placed in their buildings; none was found.

Civil aviation bore the sharpest disruption. More than 50 flights at about 15 airlines received hoax threats between 2 and 6 November, forcing diversions, emergency landings and military escorts. The air traffic authority counted 14 flights threatened on Thursday and seven on Tuesday. In Hungary, fighter jets escorted three passenger planes that received threats, and France reported four threats against aircraft over its territory. A Wizz Air flight from Prague to Rome diverted to Venice and an Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome diverted to France. No explosive has been found, no one has been injured, and overall disruption was minimal.

The campaign is larger than the aviation incidents. An investigation published on Tuesday put the total above 8,000 fake bomb warnings in the past month. It began on 19 October, when about 300 schools in Northern Ireland, at least 33 in Scotland and around 25 in Bulgaria got a bomb claim first sent to a Belfast primary school. The messages shared the subject line 'Death is near' and came from at least four hijacked email accounts. On 11 November about 6,300 schools in Italy, around 1,000 in Scotland and 300 each in Northern Ireland and Ireland were hit. The November messages were sent in the name of an obscure Russian anti-feminist group whose founder denied involvement.

American embassies across Europe issued a coordinated alert on Monday warning US citizens of possible terror attacks at crowded holiday events, citing the hoax wave. Embassies in London, Berlin, Madrid, Tallinn and Pristina named markets, transport hubs, malls and places of worship, but no specific plot, city or date. Pristina's embassy noted such alerts are routine pre-holiday guidance; Britain's terrorism threat level remains at severe. Who is behind the campaign is still unknown; France's interior minister has said foreign interference was a clear possibility, and Europe's police agency is seeking the source.

Key points

  • Broadcasters were targeted on 6 November: RTE evacuated its Dublin headquarters, TF1 and Nova were also hit, and at least 12 media organisations received threats; an email claimed to come from the leader behind the plane hoaxes.
  • More than 50 flights at about 15 airlines received hoax threats between 2 and 6 November; fighter jets escorted three planes in Hungary and two flights were diverted, to Venice and France.
  • The campaign totals more than 8,000 fake bomb warnings in the past month, mostly aimed at schools; no explosive has been found in any incident and the sender remains unidentified.
  • US embassies warned of possible terror attacks at crowded holiday events, citing the hoax wave, but named no specific plot, city or date.
