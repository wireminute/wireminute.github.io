Senate Rejects Curbs on Iran Strikes as Risk of New US Attacks Grows

The United States Senate has voted down a measure that would have forced the president to seek Congress's approval before ordering further military action against Iran, as Western officials say the chances of another round of heavy strikes have grown amid escalating skirmishes around the Gulf.

The Senate has voted down a measure that would have forced the president to seek Congress's approval before ordering further military action against Iran, as he weighs new strikes. The resolution, brought by three Democratic senators, failed by 53 votes to 47 after the chamber refused to take it up. Only one Republican, Rand Paul, backed it, while one Democrat, John Fetterman, opposed it. Even if it had passed, the president could have vetoed it, and Congress would have needed a two-thirds majority in each house to override him.

Western officials say the chances of another round of heavy strikes have grown in recent days as the two sides trade blows around the Gulf. Iran has struck American facilities in Bahrain and Kuwait several times in the past week, and on Tuesday the United States hit a missile site on Qeshm Island, at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz. Two American carrier strike groups are in the region and Washington is enforcing a naval blockade on Iran, while daily Houthi attacks in Yemen have opened a second front for Saudi Arabia.

The president is considering the move after Israeli warnings that Tehran has rebuilt its missile and drone arsenals and is advancing chemical and biological weapons programmes, according to American and Israeli officials briefed on the discussions. Netanyahu sent Mossad chief Roman Gofman to Washington last week to present the intelligence to senior officials, including CIA Director John Ratcliffe. Trump said in a television interview on Wednesday that Iran appeared to be looking at chemical and biological weapons because the nuclear route had been closed off by American and Israeli strikes. He gave no evidence, named no specific programme and said a strike was not imminent.

The administration is deeply divided over the intelligence and the wisdom of striking again, especially after midterm elections in which voters showed displeasure with a war that has pushed up prices. Possible targets include nuclear facilities, among them the underground Pickaxe Mountain site, not yet hit, while Israel's planning is also said to include energy infrastructure, which Trump has not agreed to. Diplomacy has produced little: Qatar, the main mediator, says talks are continuing but warns that escalation on the ground poses a grave danger to any deal, and Tehran wants the blockade lifted, some sanctions eased and frozen assets released before it will talk, conditions Washington has rejected.

Key points

  • The Senate has voted down a measure that would have required Congress to approve further strikes on Iran, leaving the president free to act without a vote.
  • Western officials say the chances of another round of heavy strikes have grown as the two sides trade blows around the Gulf.
  • Trump has not yet decided on new strikes; Netanyahu is pressing for a four-to-seven-day air campaign.
  • Talks are stalled: Tehran wants the blockade lifted and sanctions eased before negotiating, terms Washington has rejected.
