US Sanctions on Hezbollah-Linked Groups May Have Limited Impact, Analysts Say

New American sanctions on organisations linked to Hezbollah are unlikely to weaken the group much beyond the political message they send, analysts have said, six days after Washington announced the measures.

The United States Treasury announced on November 18 that it had sanctioned a charity working in southern Lebanon, the Wa Ta'awanu Association, along with its founder, Afif Abdel Rida Chouman, two brothers, Hassan and Ibrahim Khechen, and two gold-trading firms, Gold Global Company in Lebanon and Sankari Gold in Turkiye. Washington says the charity moves Hezbollah fighters, stores the group's weapons and channels donor money to it, while the brothers moved money from Iran's Revolutionary Guard Quds Force through gold and cash smuggling, including from Africa.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the United States would keep disrupting Iran's attempts to fund its proxies, and the State Department said the charity undermined Lebanon's sovereignty while putting legitimate aid to the south at risk. The designations freeze any assets the named people and firms hold in the United States and bar Americans from dealing with them.

But analysts say the measures will do little to change conditions inside Lebanon, where Hezbollah has been one of the dominant forces for two decades. Qassem Kassir, a Lebanese journalist with close ties to the group, said sanctions and threats of sanctions would not change conditions inside the country. He said the charity's work would carry on, just as earlier sanctions did not stop Al Qard Al Hassan, the financial institution, or Jihad Al-Binaa, the construction firm, both of which Washington has also sanctioned.

Financial expert Amir Al-Meqdad, who studied Hezbollah's pay and funding system, told Arab News the sanctions had probably reduced the group's funding, but its parallel economy, built up over 40 years, was still largely in operation. The network leans on cash and gold outside the formal banking system, which makes it hard to trace. Washington cited the February 2025 arrest of a man at Beirut airport carrying $2.5 million in cash in his hand luggage as evidence of the cash routes.

Lebanese media have reported that the United States, frustrated by the slow pace of disarming Hezbollah, could widen the sanctions to bankers, businessmen, real estate firms and contractors. Politicians, security officials and judges are unlikely to face measures yet, according to the reports. Washington has been pressing Lebanon's government to make progress on disarming the group, which has fought Israel on and off for four decades, and the sanctions are part of that pressure.

Key points

  • Washington sanctioned a southern Lebanon charity, its founder, two brothers and two gold firms on November 18, accusing them of funnelling money and support to Hezbollah.
  • Analysts say the measures will have limited effect beyond political messaging, because Hezbollah runs a parallel, cash-based economy built over 40 years.
  • The United States may widen sanctions to bankers and businessmen if Lebanon makes no progress on disarming Hezbollah, Lebanese media have reported.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Washington will keep disrupting Iran's funding of its proxies; the designations freeze US assets and bar dealings with those named.
