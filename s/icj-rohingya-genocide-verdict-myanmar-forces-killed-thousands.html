Myanmar Refuses to Fully Comply With World Court Genocide Orders

Myanmar's leader has said his government will not carry out parts of the World Court's orders on the Rohingya genocide, a week after the court ruled the state committed genocide, in his first public comments since the verdict.

Min Aung Hlaing told a meeting on Wednesday that Myanmar would take the steps appropriate for a responsible country but would not comply with requirements it considered inappropriate, according to state media. He did not say which of the court's orders he regarded as inappropriate, and he rejected the verdict, calling the case a one-sided accusation and saying the ruling had damaged the country's dignity.

The International Court of Justice ruled on 19 November, by nine votes to six, that Myanmar breached the 1948 Genocide Convention during its 2017 crackdown in northern Rakhine State. Its president, Yuji Iwasawa, read the judgment over nearly three hours, finding that the scale and nature of the violence showed an intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya group. The court said the evidence backs an estimate that 10,000 Rohingya were violently killed, and found widespread sexual violence, extreme violence against children and the systematic destruction of villages.

The judges ordered Myanmar to make reparations, including allowing the safe, voluntary and dignified return of those who fled, reuniting families and returning property lost or damaged. Myanmar must also investigate and punish anyone suspected of genocide, and report back within two years on the measures it has taken, including proposed compensation. The ruling is final and cannot be appealed, but the court has no way of enforcing it, and its orders will go to the UN Security Council.

France and the United Kingdom have asked for a closed-door meeting of the Security Council to press members on Myanmar's compliance, and legal experts note that the court's decisions are binding: under the UN Charter, every member state is legally obliged to comply with its orders, and a losing party cannot choose which parts of a judgment to accept. Myanmar's foreign ministry had already rejected the ruling as a great disappointment, pointing to the nine-to-six split.

The Gambia brought the case in November 2019 under the Genocide Convention, with the backing of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. More than 740,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh during the 2017 operations, and about 1.2 million now live as refugees there. It is only the fourth time since the Holocaust that an international court has found the crime of genocide proven, and the first time a state has been found responsible for it.

Key points

  • Myanmar's leader Min Aung Hlaing said his government will not comply with parts of the World Court's genocide orders it considers inappropriate, in his first public comments since the verdict; he did not say which orders he meant.
  • The court ruled nine to six on 19 November that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in 2017, backing an estimate of 10,000 violently killed, with widespread sexual violence and villages systematically destroyed; the ruling is final and binding, but the court cannot enforce it.
  • The court ordered Myanmar to pay reparations, allow the safe and dignified return of refugees, and investigate and punish those responsible, and to report back within two years; France and the United Kingdom have called a closed-door Security Council meeting to press compliance.
  • Legal experts say a state cannot pick which parts of a binding judgment to follow; more than 740,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh in 2017 and about 1.2 million live as refugees there, with a safe return still remote while the military holds power.
