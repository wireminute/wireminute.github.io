Europe and Japan's BepiColombo Spacecraft Reaches Mercury Orbit After Eight-Year Voyage

The European-Japanese BepiColombo spacecraft has entered orbit around Mercury, completing an eight-year journey to the solar system's smallest and innermost planet, though controllers reported a thruster problem during the arrival manoeuvre.

The European Space Agency confirmed on Saturday that the spacecraft had been captured by Mercury's gravity, beginning a months-long sequence of manoeuvres to settle it into its working orbit. In a message on Sunday, the agency said the thruster burn during the insertion was shorter than planned because of a software problem. The spacecraft is safe, and teams at the European Space Operations Centre in Germany are working to understand what happened.

BepiColombo is only the second mission to orbit Mercury, after NASA's MESSENGER, which circled the planet from 2011 to 2015, and the third mission to visit the planet at all. It is a joint project of the European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, carrying two orbiters that will separate in December: Europe's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio.

The spacecraft launched in October 2018 and travelled about 10 billion kilometres, using nine flybys of Earth, Venus and Mercury to slow down enough to be captured by the planet's gravity. The arrival was delayed by about a year after a thruster problem in 2024 left its ion engines unable to work at full power, pushing the date back from December 2025 to November 2026.

The two orbiters will stay joined until 9 and 10 December, when they separate and move into their own polar orbits. Over the following months a series of five engine burns will lower the spacecraft from its first looping orbit into its working paths. Science observations are due to begin in April 2027, with the mission planned to run for at least a year and possibly through 2029.

The mission is named after Giuseppe Colombo, the Italian mathematician and engineer who worked out the route that makes reaching Mercury's orbit possible. Its instruments will map the planet's surface, measure its magnetic field and study how the solar wind shapes the space around it, including the search for frozen water in craters at the poles that never see sunlight.

Key points

  • BepiColombo entered orbit around Mercury on Saturday 21 November, after an eight-year, roughly 10-billion-kilometre journey from its October 2018 launch.
  • It is only the second spacecraft to orbit Mercury, after NASA's MESSENGER, and the third mission to visit the planet.
  • The arrival burn ended early because of a software problem with the thrusters; the European Space Agency says the spacecraft is safe and engineers are investigating.
  • The two orbiters, Europe's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan's Mio, separate on 9 and 10 December; science work begins in April 2027.
