Review to Urge Lower Payroll Tax for Young Workers in Jobs Push

Employers should pay less payroll tax on workers aged 21 to 24, phased in over five years starting with shops, bars and restaurants, a year-long review of youth unemployment will recommend on Tuesday.

The review, led by former Labour minister Alan Milburn, will propose doubling the earnings threshold at which firms start paying National Insurance for 21- to 24-year-olds, from £5,000 to £10,000, and cutting the rate gradually rather than all at once. Research by the Resolution Foundation puts the cost of scrapping the tax for the age group immediately at £5.1bn, and Milburn will argue that, given the state of the public finances, an instant cut across the board would not be the best use of several billion pounds of public money.

Retail and hospitality would go first because they provide more than a third of jobs for 16- to 24-year-olds, 37% of them. The review was commissioned to tackle the near one million young people not in education, employment or training. Official figures put the number at just under one million in April to June, up 3% on a year earlier, and Milburn puts the annual cost of supporting economically inactive young people at £22bn.

The report will also recommend taking under-25s off the health element of Universal Credit and off Personal Independence Payment, replacing them with a youth allowance paid at the standard Universal Credit rate. Milburn will also call for a legal promise that every young person is offered a job, training or employment support. Those who refuse to engage with the support on offer could lose some of their benefits.

The government raised employer National Insurance after coming to office, and Milburn will say the rise in youth unemployment did not start with that change. The government is expected to take the recommendations into legislation in the new year. The opposition Conservatives said Milburn was right that benefits must be cut but argued their own plan went further, including halving employer National Insurance for young workers and creating 100,000 apprenticeships, while Reform said the review was pointless without immediate action.

Key points

  • Employer National Insurance for 21- to 24-year-olds would fall gradually over five years, starting in retail and hospitality.
  • The earnings threshold for the tax on that age group would double from £5,000 to £10,000.
  • Under-25s would move off sickness and disability benefits onto a youth allowance, with a legal promise of a job, training or support.
  • Just under one million 16- to 24-year-olds are not in education, employment or training, at an annual cost put at £22bn.
