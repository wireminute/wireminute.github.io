NATO Chief Vows to Defend Space After Reports of Russian Nuclear-Armed Satellite

NATO chief Mark Rutte said on Monday the alliance would defend against threats in space, after German media reported that Russia could launch a satellite carrying a nuclear warhead as early as December.

Rutte was speaking in Berlin alongside German Chancellor Friedrich Merz when he was asked about the reports. He said the alliance constantly assesses its deterrence and defence and updates it where necessary, and that it has a range of response options even if the public does not always see them. "We will always make sure that we defend every inch of NATO territory. And that includes making sure that we are safe when it comes to the space element," he said.

Merz did not confirm or deny the reports, but said he shared the concern. "Reports that Russia is planning to introduce nuclear weaponry into space are not new, yet they remain no less shocking," he said.

The investigation by broadcasters WDR and NDR and the newspaper Suddeutsche Zeitung says intelligence services of NATO member states, including Germany’s Federal Intelligence Service and its armed forces, have shared assessments concluding that Moscow plans to put the satellite, known as Sputnik-S, into orbit this year. The satellite was assembled several years ago and has been kept in a launch-ready condition in the Moscow region, the report says. Warnings exchanged among NATO states in early November indicated a launch could come within 60 days, and Germany’s military Space Command assesses that December is possible.

A nuclear explosion in orbit would threaten the satellites the modern world depends on. The US Defense Intelligence Agency said in an October assessment that Russia is developing a satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon, in breach of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which bans placing nuclear weapons in orbit, and warned there would be virtually no effective countermeasures once such a device was in space. US Space Command chief General Stephen Whiting has warned that a blast could disable about one-third of all satellites in low Earth orbit within hours or days. Russia denies the reports, calling them unfounded; Germany’s government says it is taking them seriously, and no documents or images confirming the satellite have been made public. The story is still developing.
